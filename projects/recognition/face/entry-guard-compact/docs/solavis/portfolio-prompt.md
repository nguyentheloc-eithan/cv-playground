## Problem

- Gate cameras need mains power and cables, and cheap ones only record. They do not tell the owner who is at the gate.
- A camera at a gate can be turned, covered, moved or stolen, and the owner learns about it too late.
- Selling cameras to many customers needs control: data, plans, limits, and a way to switch off a device.

## Solution

1. **Camera:** a small box with a solar panel on the sloped top, one lithium cell, a motion sensor, a camera, a white light and a 4G modem. It sleeps most of the time and wakes on motion.
2. **On-camera detection, cloud recognition:** the camera finds a face and sends only a small face crop. The cloud recognises the person against that customer's registered people and their watch list.
3. **Alerts:** in a web app that installs on a phone, and on Telegram. Watch list results always say "possible match" and need a human review.
4. **Network:** Wi-Fi first, 4G SIM as automatic backup.
5. **Tamper protection:** the camera ships unarmed, the owner arms it at the final spot, and any sign of being moved, turned, covered, opened or removed makes it take a photo, alert the owner and lock itself until the owner unlocks it.
6. **Subscription control:** plans and limits are database settings. A suspended customer's camera stops uploading on its next wake-up. Every request is checked on the server, so changed firmware gains nothing.

## Key engineering decisions (good material for the write-up)

| Decision | Why |
|---|---|
| Recognition in the cloud, not on the camera | The camera chip is too small for the full model. The camera also holds no faces and no storage keys, so a stolen camera exposes nothing. |
| Ready-made boards first, no custom PCB | Reach a pilot in about 10 weeks. A custom board is a later cost-reduction step. |
| Layered tamper detection | One sensor is never enough. A tilt sensor cannot see a pure left-right turn, so a compass, a magnet and reed switch on the cover, an image comparison on the server, and missed check-ins are combined. |
| Server is the authority | The camera cannot unlock itself. Lock state lives in the camera's flash and on the server. |
| Every camera is a "source" with a type and capabilities | Lets the same cloud later serve third-party cameras, so the second product needs no rewrite. |
| On-site agent for third-party cameras | The agent reads the customer's streams locally and sends only face crops. No open ports, no video in the cloud. |
| Row-level security per customer | Customer data is separated in the database, and automatic tests try to cross it. |
| Parametric enclosure in code | The 3D-printable box is generated by a Python script, so sizes change in one place. |

## Architecture (use this for a diagram)

Camera (ESP32-S3 with 4G) → HTTPS device API (bearer key per camera, short-lived upload links) → job queue → recognition worker (face vectors, pgvector) and tamper/health evaluator → Postgres and S3-compatible storage → alerts (web push, Telegram). Customer web app and admin console use the same API.

Stack: C++ firmware (ESP32-S3), Python and FastAPI, Postgres with pgvector, S3-compatible storage, Docker Compose, installable web app, Telegram Bot API, OpenCV-based face detection and 128-number face vectors (from the original `entry-guard-compact` project).

## Hardware facts

- Size: 125 × 90 × 79 mm including the panel (design).
- Parts (ready-made): ESP32-S3 board with LTE Cat-1 modem, OV2640 camera, AM312 PIR, accelerometer, compass, reed switch with magnet, CN3791 solar charger, 1 × 18650 cell, 6 V 1.5 W panel, white LED.
- Enclosure: wedge shape, 15° panel slope, hidden wall screws, 3 printed parts (cover, back plate, lens hood), ASA material. Generated by `hardware/enclosure/build_enclosure.py`. Not yet printed.
- Estimated energy use: about 0.5 to 0.8 Wh a day (estimate, to be measured). Estimated cost per camera: about 2.0 million VND in parts at about 10 units (estimate).
